EU × Antitrust16 Jul

EU orders Google to open Android to rival AI assistants and share Search data

Why it matters: this is a binding structural remedy, not a fine — it forces Google to give competing AI assistants the same system-level Android access Gemini gets, which resets the competitive baseline for every non-Google assistant trying to compete on phones, and signals the EU intends to police default-AI advantages the same way it once policed default-browser advantages.

Detail & sources

On July 16, the European Commission issued two sets of binding specification measures against Google under the Digital Markets Act. The first requires Google to open 11 Android feature groups — from voice activation (letting a rival assistant respond to a "Hey Google"-style hotword) to the ability to act across apps on a user's behalf — to competing AI assistants, which today only get restricted access compared with Gemini. The second requires Google to share anonymized Search data with rival search engines and AI developers, using a multi-layered anonymization method developed with privacy experts and aligned with draft DMA/GDPR guidance. Google must implement AI-assistant interoperability in Android 18 by August 1, 2027, with concurrent hotword detection following in Android 19 by August 1, 2028; the Search-data measures phase in through 2026, with final pricing due by January 2027.

Google × Roadmap15–16 Jul

Gemini 3.5 Pro misses its third launch deadline in a row

Why it matters: a slipped deadline is engineering discipline; a third slip — after Google ordered a ground-up rebuild and it still falls short — suggests something structural in either the training run or Google's own bar for reliability, and it leaves Google as the only major lab still without a 2026 flagship in general production.

Detail & sources

Google unveiled Gemini 3.5 Pro at I/O on May 19, targeting a June general-availability launch. That date slipped to July after enterprise testers flagged token-efficiency, coding-performance and multi-step-reasoning gaps. A second, more serious delay followed when Google reportedly concluded the model had structural failures in recursive tool-calling and SVG generation that fine-tuning couldn't fix, prompting a full pre-training rebuild targeting a widely leaked July 17 date. That date has now also passed: reporting from July 15–16 says the rebuilt model is still producing frequent hallucinations and falling short of internal reliability standards, and trails OpenAI's GPT-5.6 on benchmark comparisons. Google has not set a new public date.

Chips × Capex16 Jul

TSMC pledges another $100B for Arizona after a record AI-driven quarter

Why it matters: the world's dominant advanced-chip foundry is doubling down on US capacity specifically to keep pace with "leading US customers'" AI demand — a direct data point on how much runway chipmakers think the AI buildout still has, and a signal for anyone modeling when leading-edge capacity constraints might ease.

Detail & sources

TSMC announced an additional $100 billion investment in Arizona on July 16, on top of the $165 billion pledged in March 2025, bringing its total US commitment to $265 billion across roughly 14 facilities: 10 fabs, two advanced-packaging plants and an R&D center. CEO C.C. Wei said the new money funds four more fabs at 2-nanometer-and-below process nodes plus advanced packaging, "to support the strong multi-year demand from our leading US customers." The pledge followed TSMC's Q2 earnings, which posted a record 77.4% year-over-year profit jump on $40.2B in revenue; the company raised its 2026 capex guidance to $60–64B (from $52–56B) and its full-year revenue-growth guidance to slightly above 40% in USD terms.

Memory × Supply Chain19 Jul

SK Group's Chey Tae-won: AI memory shortage is becoming a geopolitical problem

Why it matters: a 60–100% projected jump in AI memory demand for 2027 with essentially no new capacity coming online is a hard capacity ceiling on every roadmap that assumes GPUs and HBM will just be available — and a memory chief warning that governments will start pressuring each other over access is a signal this shortage could turn into an export-control-style fight, the way advanced logic chips already have.

Detail & sources

Speaking at the Korea Chamber of Commerce and Industry's Jeju Forum on July 19, SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won said foreign governments increasingly treat AI memory access as a matter of "economic security." He warned that customers are asking for 60–100% more AI memory in 2027 than in 2026, while "no company has meaningful new capacity coming online next year." SK Hynix, which Chey chairs, controls roughly 58% of the global HBM market. "Right now, companies absorb the pressure," he said. "Governments will start pressuring other governments soon." Earlier in the same forum he forecast overall chip demand growing at least 50% next year against roughly flat supply.

Huawei × Chips17 Jul

Huawei confirms Atlas 950 SuperPoD push into South Korea as an Nvidia alternative

Why it matters: Huawei is now marketing directly into a US ally's AI-infrastructure market with a price/performance pitch aimed squarely at Nvidia's export-restricted H20 — worth watching for any organization sourcing AI compute in Asia, since it's a live test of whether Chinese silicon can win share where Nvidia is legally allowed to sell but priced or supply-constrained out.

Detail & sources

Huawei confirmed plans to bring its Atlas 950 SuperPoD and Ascend AI chips to South Korea, targeting a Q4 2026 launch. Deployment-scale clusters pack up to 8,192 Ascend 950 accelerators, and Huawei claims roughly triple the inference performance of Nvidia's China-market H20 chip at about one-quarter the cost. The move follows Huawei's broader Atlas 950 rollout (up to 8 EFLOPS FP8 / 16 EFLOPS FP4 in full configuration) and comes as South Korean firms weigh Chinese AI hardware against Nvidia's supply constraints and pricing for large-scale buildouts.

AI Security × Incident Response16 Jul

Hugging Face discloses a breach carried out end-to-end by an autonomous AI agent — and says US model guardrails blocked its own investigation

Why it matters: this is one of the clearest public examples yet of a fully agentic attack chain, and the twist — defenders locked out of frontier-model help by the same safety guardrails meant to stop misuse — is a preview of a real operational gap: incident responders analyzing exploit code and C2 artifacts can look identical to an attacker to a safety classifier, and security teams need a sanctioned path around that before their next incident, not during it.

Detail & sources

Hugging Face disclosed on July 16 that a production breach of its data-processing pipeline was executed entirely by an autonomous AI agent framework. A malicious dataset exploited code-execution flaws in a remote dataset loader and a configuration-template injection to gain a foothold on a processing worker; from there, the agent escalated privileges, harvested cloud and cluster credentials, and moved laterally across internal infrastructure over a weekend, executing tens of thousands of automated actions from a swarm of short-lived sandboxes and staging command-and-control on public services. Hugging Face says internal datasets and some service credentials were compromised, but public-facing models, user data and the software supply chain were verified intact. Notably, the company's blue team first tried commercial frontier-model APIs to analyze the attack logs, but those requests were blocked by the providers' safety guardrails, which "cannot distinguish an incident responder from an attacker." The team instead ran GLM 5.2, an open-weight model from China's Z.ai, on its own infrastructure to process more than 17,000 attacker logs and reconstruct the timeline.

GitHub × Pricing20 Jul

GitHub Code Quality moves from preview to a paid, per-committer product today

Why it matters: more than 10,000 enterprises used Code Quality for free during preview; today it starts billing at $10 per active committer per month on enabled repos, plus consumption charges for AI-powered checks and CodeQL's use of Actions minutes — teams that didn't explicitly audit and scope which repos have it enabled are about to get a surprise line item.

Detail & sources

GitHub Code Quality reaches general availability on July 20 as a purchasable product, priced at $10 per active committer per month on repositories where it's enabled, plus usage-based charges for AI-powered capabilities such as Copilot code review, AI-assisted detection and Copilot Autofix; deterministic CodeQL analysis consumes GitHub Actions minutes. New GA capabilities include organization-wide deployment, org-level quality dashboards, code-coverage enforcement via rulesets, and repository- and organization-level quality scoring. GitHub shipped a license-estimate tool in preview on July 13 specifically so admins could see their active-committer counts and forecast the bill before billing started.
