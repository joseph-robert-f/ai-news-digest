Moonshot's 2.8-trillion-parameter Kimi K3 closes the gap with Claude Fable 5

Why it matters: the largest open-weight model ever released now claims to beat GPT-5.6 Sol and Opus 4.8 on coding benchmarks while running competitively with the current frontier — raising the bar for what "free and good enough" means for any team weighing a self-hosted model against a frontier API, and doing it despite US chip export controls.

Detail & sources

Beijing-based Moonshot AI released Kimi K3, a 2.8-trillion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts model (896 experts, 16 active per token) built on Kimi Delta Attention hybrid linear attention, with native vision understanding and up to a 1M-token context window. Moonshot says K3 performs "competitively" with Anthropic's Fable 5 and "substantially outperforms" Anthropic's Opus 4.8 and OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-5.5 on its internal evaluations; independent leaderboard results also placed it ahead of both on head-to-head coding-arena comparisons. Full model weights are due July 27. Axios framed the release as evidence China has "erased" much of the US's perceived AI lead despite ongoing chip restrictions, and the news moved AI-adjacent equities the same day.

FTC × State AI Law1–17 Jul, comments through 31 Jul

FTC's bid to preempt Colorado's AI Act enters its final comment stretch

Why it matters: if the FTC's implied-preemption theory holds, any state law that forces a company to alter a model's truthful outputs — bias audits, disparate-impact mandates — becomes federally challengeable as consumer deception, which is a live legal risk for every compliance team currently building to state-specific AI guardrails rather than a single federal standard.

Detail & sources

On July 1, the FTC voted 2-0 to publish a proposed policy statement on the "suppression of accuracy in artificial intelligence systems," arguing that state laws compelling AI developers to alter truthful model outputs — even to comply with the law — may itself be a deceptive act under Section 5 of the FTC Act. The statement singles out Colorado's revised AI Act (SB 26-189, effective January 1, 2027) as an example, and argues such state law is "impliedly preempted" to the extent it conflicts with the federal scheme; a company's motive for altering outputs to comply with state law is "irrelevant" to a Section 5 violation, per the FTC. One compliance path the agency floats: clear, prominent disclosure that a system's outputs are steered to prioritize state-law compliance over accuracy. The public comment period runs through July 31, 2026.

Vulnerability Management17 Jul

WordPress patches "wp2shell," a critical pre-auth RCE hittable on default installs

Why it matters: no login, no plugin and no special configuration are needed — a default WordPress install is exploitable by a single anonymous HTTP request, which makes this a same-day patch priority for the roughly 40% of the web still running WordPress core, not a routine update to queue for later.

Detail & sources

WordPress shipped 7.0.2 and 6.9.5 on July 17, fixing a flaw researchers dubbed wp2shell: CVE-2026-63030, a pre-authentication remote-code-execution bug that chains a REST API batch-request route-confusion issue with SQL injection, plus a related standalone SQL-injection flaw, CVE-2026-60137, affecting WordPress 6.8 and above. The RCE chain hits WordPress 6.9.0–6.9.4 and 7.0.0–7.0.1; sites on older branches can patch via 6.8.6. Cloudflare shipped WAF rules to shield unpatched sites in the interim.

Supply Chain × Vulnerability17 Jul

OpenSSL quietly fixes "HollowByte," an 11-byte flaw that can exhaust server memory — with no CVE

Why it matters: the bug hits nearly every TLS-terminating stack — Node, Python, Ruby, PHP, MySQL, Postgres, NGINX, Apache — and shipped with no CVE, advisory or changelog entry, so it won't show up in a routine dependency scan; if you haven't explicitly upgraded to a patched OpenSSL build, you're carrying an untracked memory-exhaustion risk.

Detail & sources

Okta security researchers detailed HollowByte: older OpenSSL versions pre-allocate a receive buffer sized to a connection's declared (but unverified) length header — up to 131KB for an inbound ClientHello — before a single body byte arrives. Combined with glibc's tendency to hold onto freed small-to-medium allocations rather than returning them to the OS, an attacker opening waves of connections with randomized declared sizes fragments the heap and drives up resident memory without ever crossing a connection-count limit. Okta's own testing OOM-killed a 1GB server using just 547MB of frozen fragments, and locked up 25% of memory on a 16GB box, using payloads as small as 11 bytes. OpenSSL silently fixed the issue in 4.0.1, with silent backports to 3.6.3, 3.5.7, 3.4.6 and 3.0.21 — without assigning a CVE.
