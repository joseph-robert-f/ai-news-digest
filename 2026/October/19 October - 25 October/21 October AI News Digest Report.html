Security19 Oct

One hacker used AI agents to breach 100+ company sites for about $50 each

Why it matters: Attack cost has fallen to a few dollars of tokens per site, so patch cycles and exposed admin panels now matter more than attacker skill.

Detail & sources

Reuters reports that a Chinese-speaking hacker used Cairn, an open-source tool that coordinates AI agents, to breach more than 100 company websites. NorForge researcher Snorre Fagerland found exposed Cairn control panels and saw over 1,200 sites targeted. He estimates each successful intrusion took about 23 hours and about $50 in tokens. Named victims include Deere, SharkNinja and Cummins. Deere saw no sign sensitive data was accessed; SharkNinja said only a standalone support site was hit. Reuters could not establish how much data was taken. Reports differ on which AI models the agents used, so we do not name them.

Agents & security19 Oct

Microsoft previews runtime controls that cut an AI agent’s rights as its risk rises

Why it matters: Security teams get a way to grant agents narrow, expiring access, which may decide how fast firms let agents touch real systems.

Detail & sources

Microsoft extends Entra Conditional Access to agents. An agent at higher risk can first lose write access, then need human approval for high-impact actions, and at the top of the scale be isolated or stopped. Dynamic Permissions give an agent only what one task needs, and the access ends with the task. Monitoring runs outside the agent’s execution container so a compromised agent cannot switch it off. The features are in private preview; broader availability is planned for Microsoft’s November Ignite conference, per the reports.

Policy19 Oct

US orders civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek from government systems

Why it matters: Any vendor that embeds DeepSeek models may now be unusable for federal civilian work, so check supply chains for hidden dependencies.

Detail & sources

The Department of Homeland Security order, dated 24 July and signed by Secretary Markwayne Mullin, covers DeepSeek products and third-party services built on its models. It rests on the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act of 2018 and was posted to the Federal Register on 19 October. DHS cites the risk that user data could reach Beijing under Chinese security law, and warns the models may hide malware or flaws. The order covers civilian agencies only; private firms and individuals are not affected. We found no statement from Beijing in the coverage; the Chinese embassy and DeepSeek did not comment to outlets.
