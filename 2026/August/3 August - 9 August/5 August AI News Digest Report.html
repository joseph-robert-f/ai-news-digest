npm × ChainDrop3–4 Aug

A self-propagating npm worm hits 400+ packages — and starts targeting AI-IDE configs

Why it matters: this is an active, ongoing supply-chain incident touching packages with billions of combined monthly downloads, and its move to plant itself in AI-assisted-IDE configuration is a new escalation any team using Claude Code, Copilot, or similar tools should check for now, not after the next sprint.

Detail & sources

A worm researchers are calling ChainDrop (also referenced as "Mini Shai-Hulud") hijacked the GitHub account of the maintainer behind the widely used keyv and cacheable npm namespaces and used it to publish malicious versions of over 400 packages — including keyv, cacheable, flat-cache, and file-entry-cache — with combined downloads exceeding 2 billion per month. On August 4 (UTC) it poisoned 444 packages across 2,212 versions in under four hours, hitting victim organizations including Deliveroo, Ornikar, and ServiceTitan. Each compromised package drops an obfuscated Bun-runtime payload via a preinstall hook that steals npm, GitHub, AWS, Kubernetes, Vault, Slack, and Stripe credentials plus SSH keys, then self-propagates using the stolen npm tokens; Microsoft and Wiz both flagged that the payload also writes into Claude and VS Code configuration files as a secondary infection vector. Teams with these packages in their dependency tree should audit lockfiles and rotate credentials from a clean host rather than wait for a full postmortem.

Amazon × Perplexity4 Aug

Ninth Circuit rules AI shopping agents don't "hack" websites, vacates Amazon's injunction against Comet

Why it matters: this is the first federal appellate ruling on whether an autonomous AI agent can legally act on a user's behalf across a third party's website, and it significantly weakens platforms' ability to use anti-hacking law to block competing agentic browsers — a direct precedent for anyone building agentic commerce tools.

Detail & sources

A three-judge Ninth Circuit panel vacated the preliminary injunction Amazon had won against Perplexity's Comet browser, which logs into a user's account and completes purchases on Amazon.com on their behalf. Amazon had argued Comet violated the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, the federal anti-hacking statute, by accessing Amazon's systems without authorization. The panel held that it is the user — not Perplexity — who "accesses" Amazon's computers, since Comet only processes screenshots the user's own browser already captured, and applied the rule of lenity to construe the criminal statute against liability.

Why it matters: it's the first DOJ immigration-hiring enforcement action against a major AI lab, and it arrives alongside the administration's broader "AI jobs for Americans" push — a template other agencies may now apply to AI-sector hiring practices more broadly.

Detail & sources

The DOJ's Civil Rights Division announced a combined $3.2 million settlement with OpenAI OpCo LLC and its subsidiary Statsig, resolving findings that both companies violated the Immigration and Nationality Act by favoring temporary-visa holders over qualified U.S. workers during PERM labor-certification hiring.
